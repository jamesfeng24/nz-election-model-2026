Pawn to Queen – and the unspoken clash of the campaign

Both the poll and the pow-wow show Opportunity leader Qiulae Wong trailing in pivotal Mt Albert electorate – she tells Newsroom her strategy from now
by Jonathan Milne 01/10/202601/10/2026

Campaign observer: Asked at the start of Mt Albert electorate debate who would like to be Prime Minister, only one of the six candidates put up her hand. Qiulae Wong.

It distilled down the essence of the debate before it had even got going. For all six of the candidates there, it was about winning party vote. For three, it was also about winning the electorate.

But only for the Opportunity leader was it about establishing a political future at the heart of government. And to that point, Wednesday evening’s Spinoff debate highlighted an unspoken clash that Newsroom put to Wong afterwards.

At that point, she was still looking somewhat subdued, after The Spinoff announced the results of its Curia poll of 400 Mt Albert voters, showing Labour incumbent Helen White on 33 percent, just a one-point nudge ahead of National list MP Melissa Lee. The Greens’ Ricardo Menéndez March was third on 14 percent, and Wong trailed on 10 percent.

The Opportunity leader is running a two-tick campaign in Mt Albert. She had dared to dream she could win the seat to secure the up-and-coming party a backdoor entry into Parliament, if it fails to cross MMP’s 5 percent threshold.

On the strength of this week’s poll, that hope might now look forlorn. But electorates are small and notoriously difficult to reliably poll. So she’s not allowing that result to dictate her strategy.

What was perhaps more worrying still was her debate execution.

Because most of those in the audience were media and campaign workers, not locals. In truth, they wanted to see how the newcomer would perform. She did not appoint.

I mean, sure, technically, she turned up to the appointment. She and the party’s general manager Iain Lees-Galloway were among the first to arrive at The Spinoff’s venue in the old Mount Albert Borough Council Chambers.

But it felt like she was there in body, not soul. As the other five candidates chipped and gibed and talked over each other to make their points and enforce their dominance, she sat quietly.

When she spoke, she was clear and on message. But she spoke only when spoken to by the moderator, Toby Manhire.

Because the debate was focused on Mt Albert, the question was never asked whether Wong could work with any of the other candidates in Parliament or specifically, in government. Christopher Luxon and Winston Peters have derided Opportunity, but neither Wong nor the candidates from the governing coalition was asked about that unspoken clash.

Perhaps this is consistent with Opportunity’s determination to sit above the “tribal warfare” and “outdated left and right boxing match” of left-right politics; the effect was that she sat outside the debate entirely.

That opened the floor to the frontrunners to dominate. Melissa Lee was on-message and emphatic. Ricardo Menéndez March sparkled. Sheldon Eden-Whaitiri was surefooted and entertaining and will hope for a good ranking on NZ First’s party list. Act’s Alex Price was smart and clear about his party’s positioning.

But the last word, in defining the contest, went to Helen White.

‘It isn’t about an insurance policy’

This seat, in various incarnations, has an 80-year history of Labour representation. No fewer than five former Labour prime ministers were lauded in the debate. Michael Joseph Savage, Norman Kirk, Helen Clark, Jacinda Ardern, and Chris Hipkins.

As Auckland West, Savage held the seat from 1919 until his death in 1940. It was Clark’s electorate. It was Ardern’s electorate.

Melissa Lee almost took it off White at the last election, riding a blue wave. But White hung on by an insanely narrow margin of 18 votes.

And since then, the Labour MP insists, she’s been working hard on local issues. Housing. Health. Schools. Cost of living.

“It needs somebody locally focused, actually championing what we need. And that’s not about using Mount Albert as a pawn in some big game. It isn’t about an insurance policy. It’s about making sure that this area has a really good, focused MP.”

The implicit accusation: that Qiulae Wong in her pursuit of the political crown, is using Mt Albert as a dispensable pawn on her path to Parliament.

‘Local champion’

Both White and Wong spoke to Newsroom after the debate. Unsurprisingly, one placed more weight on the poll result than the other.

“The poll confirms my thinking,” White says. “That splitting the vote could well lead to an unintended outcome of the seat going to Melissa Lee who does not live in the electorate, when the majority would love a progressive representative and a local to be their local champion.”

Despite Mt Albert’s legacy of delivering leaders to Parliament, it’s not Savage or Clark or Ardern who White wants to talk about. She wants to talk about the importance of having a hardworking local MP who isn’t distracted by leadership on the national stage.

“I love the legacy of this seat, which has been Labour for 80 years. But for me it’s about values and advocating for this amazing area where I live. Warren Freer was the MP here and a great representative and was never leader,” she says.

“Leaders actually have a disadvantage because they can’t favour their own electorate while doing that job – but I can be as zealous an advocate as is necessary.”

She argues MMP is structured to elect local champions first, and top up with list MPs to deliver proportionality, because constituency MPs bring something to politics that is incredibly valuable: wisdom from their electorate, to ground national decision-making. “I get to see everyday experiences and I bring that to Parliament.”

But Wong argues she can do both – lead Opportunity into a governing coalition/support arrangement and represent Mt Albert.

She professes to be unfazed by the Curia poll result. “An electorate poll is always so hard to know exactly what it means. And as a newcomer into the race, there are so many things that influence that,” she says.

“We’re getting good feedback on the doorstep, and we’re just going to keep getting out there. I’m really passionate about Mt Albert. I live here, and I really care about the community, and I think that I could be a great local MP.”

Wong had initially focused on campaigning nationwide. Now she’s focusing on campaigning in urban areas, Monday to Friday, while her deputy Daniel Eb leverages his farming background to target the provinces.

At the weekends, she’s wholly focused on Mt Albert – and says that’s not about to change off the back of one poll of 400 voters.

Bottom lines and bottom-trawling

Earlier this week, Wong laid out the party’s five so-called bottom lines for post-election talks. While most were fairly non-contentious and wouldn’t provide any impediment to negotiations with any party, there were two that put Opportunity at loggerheads with NZ First and Act, in particular.

Those were the insistence that any government they backed recommit to the Paris Agreement climate obligation, and the requirement to immediately phase out bottom trawling.

The latter came under the headline, “revive ocean health”, but Wong now clarifies that it’s not about the oceans. In the first instance, it’s just about phasing out bottom-trawling in the Hauraki Gulf and other sensitive inshore areas. Seamounts might be included, where they are sensitive.

“I think it’s very practical, and that’s all we’re asking for in the bottom line: an immediate ban on sensitive areas like the Hauraki Gulf. It’s not the full policy. We’re not committing right now to when we’re going to ban it completely, but we do think it’s important to take that first.”

She walks back, somewhat, comments from the party’s oceans spokesperson, Dr Kayla Kingdon-Bebb, who had said the phased ban would extend out beyond this country’s exclusive economic zone to the deep international waters of the South Pacific.

That may be the party’s policy, but it’s not a bottom line, Wong says.

“That’s what we think we can achieve in the next three years. We are realistic that a full phase-out is going to take some time in collaboration with industry, because we recognise that 70 percent of our current fishing industry does rely on bottom-trawling. We don’t accept that it should be that way forever, but but we know that we need to work with industry and iwi on that.”

To that extent, she thinks there’s nothing in the party’s five bottom lines that should provide an insurmountable hurdle to working with any other party in Parliament – even with NZ First.

Can Opportunity really be colour-blind?

This is where we get to the unspoken clash.

Because, watching from the back of Mt Albert Borough Council Chambers, we could see National Party campaign workers in their bright royal blue jackets sitting close to Opportunity supporters in their more-blue-than-teal tshirts.

At risk of being flippant, these two can never work together, surely? The two colours clash terribly.

As someone who says she grew up wanting to be a fashion designer, before turning her attention to becoming prime minister, Qiulae Wong must know this deep down.

But she’s not saying. Instead, she just laughs at the question. “Well, if they can work with our bottom lines, that is the most important thing, and that’s why we put those out there.”

Opportunity’s policies of paying everyone a universal basic income, paid largely from the proceeds of a new land tax, are at the heart and soul of the party’s manifesto – but they’re not bottom lines. Pursuing those would categorically rule out working with National and its coalition partners.

She knows the question of colour coordination is really about something more serious, and she’s insistent on continuing to portray Opportunity as colour-blind.

“Regardless of whether it’s a Labour or National-led government, we’re being clear on the things that we want to achieve. Ultimately, that’s up to them if they want to work with us on that.”
